# The Battle of Colachel (1741): Script

Narration only. Shot numbers in [brackets] match `Colachel_Shot_List.md`.
Target read: about 11 minutes at a calm documentary pace (about 150 words a minute).
Every fact is checked against the sources in `Sources.md`. Disputed points are flagged in the narration itself.

---

## HOOK

[S01] A fishing village called Colachel, at the southern tip of India. August, seventeen forty-one.

[S02] On the beach stands a wall of sharpened wooden posts. Behind it wait the soldiers of the Dutch East India Company, a trading company from the Netherlands with its own ships, its own forts, and its own army.

[S03] Outside the wall is the army of Travancore, a small kingdom on the south-west coast of India. Its king is Marthanda Varma. He is thirty-six years old.

[S04] The Dutch came here to punish him. They have ships, cannons and trained musketeers. Two years ago, their governor told this king that the Company could crush him.

[S05] Now the Dutch are the ones who are trapped. Behind them, the monsoon sea is too rough for ships to come close. Inside the wall, the rice is running low. And the king outside is in no hurry.

[S06] Soon, a single cannon shot will end this siege. And the Dutch commander will spend the rest of his life working for the king he came to beat.

So, how did a trading company end up trapped on a beach, a short march from a king's palace? It starts with pepper.

## SEGMENT 1: THE PEPPER COAST

[S07] Kerala, the early seventeen hundreds. Picture a map, with north at the top.

[S08] A long, thin strip of land runs down the west coast of India. On one side is the Arabian Sea. On the other is a wall of green hills called the Western Ghats. Between them, the land is wet, thick with coconut palms, and cut by rivers.

[S09] On those hills grows black pepper, a small dried berry that Europeans pay high prices for. Ships have come for it for centuries.

[S10] The coast is split into many small kingdoms. Kochi, Kollam, Kayamkulam, and a crowd of tiny ones, each with its own raja, a local king.

[S11] The Dutch hold the fort at Kochi. They took it from the Portuguese in sixteen sixty-three. On our map, Dutch forts are orange.

[S12] Their method is simple. They sign contracts with the small rajas. Sell your pepper only to us, at our price. And because each raja is small, none of them is strong enough to refuse.

Remember that. The whole Dutch plan depends on the kings of this coast staying small.

## SEGMENT 2: THE KING WHO GREW

[S13] Now look south, to the very tip of the strip. Meet Marthanda Varma.

[S14] He is born in seventeen oh-five. In seventeen twenty-nine, at about twenty-four, he becomes the ruler of Travancore. His capital is Padmanabhapuram, a palace town built of carved wood, about thirteen kilometres from the sea.

[S15] Travancore is small. And his own nobles are not loyal. He spends his first years fighting powerful families at home who do not want him on the throne. He wins.

[S16] Then he turns north. In the seventeen thirties, his army takes one neighbour after another. Attingal. The hill country of Nedumangad. Kottarakkara.

[S17] And here is the problem for the Dutch. Each of those little kingdoms had signed a pepper contract with them. Once the land belongs to Travancore, the contract is gone. The pepper goes to whoever pays the most, and often that means the English.

[S18] The Dutch needed the kings to stay small. This one is getting bigger every year.

## SEGMENT 3: THE THREAT

[S19] So, in seventeen thirty-nine, the Dutch send an important man. Meet Gustaaf Willem van Imhoff, the governor of Ceylon, the island known today as Sri Lanka.

[S20] Van Imhoff comes to Kochi to fix the pepper problem. He writes that Dutch business on this coast is in complete ruin. And he believes the answer is force.

[S21] He demands that Marthanda Varma give back one of the kingdoms he has taken, and return it to its exiled princess. If the king refuses, the Dutch will invade Travancore.

[S22] The king's answer has become a famous story in Kerala. The historian M. O. Koshy recorded it like this. The king says that if the Dutch come, he will go into the forests. Van Imhoff says the Dutch will follow him there. And the king replies that he has been thinking about invading Europe one day.

[S23] The story has been told many ways, and it may have grown in the telling. But the meaning is clear. The king is not afraid.

[S24] Van Imhoff leaves. In November seventeen thirty-nine, the Dutch in Kerala declare war. They do it without permission from their own bosses in Batavia, the Company's capital in Asia, which is Jakarta today.

## SEGMENT 4: THE FIRST ROUND

[S25] At first, the war goes the Dutch way. The Dutch and their allies, the rajas of Kollam, Kayamkulam and Kochi, march on the Travancore army near Kollam.

[S26] An exploding shell lands on the Travancore gunpowder store. It blows up. The Travancore soldiers run, and the Dutch capture sixteen cannons.

Remember that gunpowder store.

[S27] The allies push south toward Attingal and Varkala. But at Varkala they meet about five thousand Nairs, the warrior families of Kerala, men trained to fight with sword and shield from boyhood. The allies cannot break through, and they pull back.

[S28] So, for most of seventeen forty, the war in the north goes back and forth. Neither side can finish it.

[S29] Then, in late seventeen forty, the Dutch get about one hundred and seventy-five fresh soldiers from Ceylon. And they make a new plan. Stop fighting the king's army in the north. Strike him at home, in the south.

## SEGMENT 5: THE LANDING

[S30] On the twenty-sixth of November, seventeen forty, Dutch warships appear off the coast near Colachel. They fire on the shore. Three days later, the Dutch announce a blockade. No ship may trade with Travancore's coast.

[S31] Then, in February seventeen forty-one, seven large ships and a crowd of smaller boats come in close. Soldiers wade ashore on the sand just north of Colachel.

[S32] Meet their commander, Eustachius De Lannoy. He is about twenty-five, a Catholic officer from a family of the borderlands between France and Flanders. His speciality is building forts.

[S33] Now look at the ground. Colachel is a fishing village on a long sandy beach. Behind it are rice fields and palm groves. About thirteen kilometres inland is Padmanabhapuram, the king's palace.

[S34] On the beach, the Dutch build a stockade, a wall of thick wooden posts driven into the sand. Then they march inland. Village after village falls, all the way to Kottar.

[S35] How big is their force? The records do not give a clear total. Perhaps a few hundred European soldiers, with local troops beside them. It is a small army. But the king and most of his men are far away in the north.

And now the Dutch are close to his palace.

## SEGMENT 6: THE KING COMES HOME

[S36] Marthanda Varma rushes south. At Kalkulam, near his capital, he raises about two thousand more Nair fighters, on top of his regular soldiers.

[S37] Legend says that before the march, he goes to the temple of Adikesava Perumal at Thiruvattar, and lays his sword before the god. Some accounts date the march to the twenty-seventh of May, seventeen forty-one.

[S38] Then the weather changes. In June, the south-west monsoon arrives. Heavy rain, strong winds, and a sea that throws waves onto the beach day and night. For months, big ships cannot safely come near this coast.

[S39] Think about what that means for the Dutch. Their help is in Ceylon, across the sea. And their bosses in Batavia have a war of their own in Java. No one is coming.

[S40] The Travancore army pushes the Dutch out of the villages. Back to the coast. Back behind the wooden wall at Colachel.

[S41] Then the king does something simple. He surrounds the stockade, and waits. This is a siege: an army camps around a fort and cuts it off until the men inside give up.

## SEGMENT 7: THE FISHERMEN

[S42] The Dutch have one more problem. The people of the beach are against them.

[S43] Colachel belongs to the Mukkuvar, a fishing community of the southern coast. De Lannoy himself later wrote an account of the war. In it, the fishermen refuse Dutch bribes, delay Dutch help, and lead Dutch soldiers the wrong way.

[S44] Kerala tells one more story. The fishermen set up palm tree trunks on carts, shaped and painted like cannons. From the sea, the Travancore army looks far bigger than it is.

[S45] That story is still told today. No record from the time proves it.

[S46] Two small Dutch ships try to sail in with help. The Travancore guns open fire, and the ships turn back to sea.

## SEGMENT 8: ONE SHOT

[S47] June becomes July. Inside the stockade, the men count their rice. Outside, the rain keeps falling.

[S48] The fifth of August, seventeen forty-one. A Travancore gun fires at the stockade, the same as every other day.

[S49] But this ball lands on a barrel of gunpowder. The historian M. O. Koshy wrote that a burning hot shot hit the gunpowder barrel, by accident.

[S50] The barrel explodes. Fire spreads through the camp. The rice stores burn. Koshy wrote that the fire went on for two days.

[S51] You know this one. Near Kollam, a lucky shot into a gunpowder store broke the Travancore army. Now a lucky shot breaks the Dutch.

[S52] No food. Very little powder. No ships. The Dutch Company's own records say the garrison surrendered on the seventh of August. In Kerala, the day is remembered as the tenth. Historians still argue over which date is right.

[S53] About twenty-four Dutch officers are taken prisoner, among them De Lannoy and his deputy, Donadi. Some historians say the surrender terms promised the Dutch safe passage, with their weapons, and that the king did not keep that promise.

[S54] The prisoners are marched inland to Udayagiri, a fort in the hills near the capital.

## SEGMENT 9: THE PRISONER WHO STAYED

[S55] Now comes the strangest part of the story. The king offers De Lannoy a job.

[S56] Train my army, he says, the way Europeans fight. De Lannoy says yes.

[S57] He drills the Travancore soldiers to fire muskets in lines, and to work cannon. He sets up a works to make iron cannonballs. He rebuilds the fort at Udayagiri, the same fort where he was a prisoner.

[S58] The king gives him a title. Valiya Kappithan, which means the Great Captain.

[S59] With his trained army, Travancore takes Kollam, then Kayamkulam, and keeps going north. De Lannoy also designs the Nedumkotta, a long line of walls and ditches across the north of the kingdom. Decades later, those lines will stand in the way of Tipu Sultan of Mysore.

[S60] De Lannoy serves Travancore for about thirty-seven years. He dies in seventeen seventy-seven, and he is buried in a small chapel inside Udayagiri fort. His tomb is still there.

## SEGMENT 10: THE END OF THE DUTCH PLAN

[S61] The war does not end at Colachel. The Dutch hold Kanyakumari, the southern tip of India, until October seventeen forty-one, and then leave. Peace talks drag on for years.

[S62] In seventeen fifty-three, the two sides sign the Treaty of Mavelikkara. The Dutch promise not to stand in the king's way. They even agree to sell him guns and powder.

[S63] Remember the Dutch plan? It needed the kings of this coast to stay small. After Colachel, Marthanda Varma becomes the biggest king on the coast, and the pepper trade passes into his hands.

[S64] In seventeen fifty, he does something unusual. He gives his whole kingdom to Padmanabha, the god of the great temple at Thiruvananthapuram, and rules from then on as the god's servant.

[S65] Today, a victory pillar stands on the beach at Colachel. The Indian Army's Madras Regiment traces part of its history to the Nair soldiers of Travancore. And at the army camp at Pangode, the parade ground is called Kulachal Ground.

## ENDING

[S66] So, why did the Dutch lose at Colachel?

A small force landed far from home. The monsoon cut it off from the sea. The fishing people worked against it. A patient king starved it inside its own wall. And one burning shot found the gunpowder.

[S67] In India, Colachel is often called the first time an Asian kingdom beat a European power. That claim is too big. The Mughals had already beaten the Portuguese, and in China, Koxinga drove the Dutch out of Taiwan in sixteen sixty-two. But Colachel did something unusual. It ended Dutch plans for this coast, and it turned the enemy commander into the king's own general.

[S68] A beach. A wooden wall. One shot into a powder barrel. And a Dutch captain who stayed for thirty-seven years.
