# Hydaspes: Sources and fact notes

Battle of the Hydaspes (Jhelum), 326 BCE. Alexander III of Macedon against Porus, king of the land between the Hydaspes (Jhelum) and Acesines (Chenab), in Punjab (today in Pakistan).

**How to read the evidence.** Every detailed account of this battle is Greek or Roman, written about 300 to 450 years later. Those writers used eyewitness histories that are now lost: Ptolemy (a general there, later king of Egypt) and Aristobulus (an engineer with the army), both used by Arrian, plus Onesicritus and Nearchus. Callisthenes, the court historian, died in 327 BCE, before the battle. No Indian text from the time names Alexander or Porus. So "the Greek version" is not one source. It is several later authors who sometimes disagree with each other, and the script should say where they do.

- Peter Green, *Alexander of Macedon 356-323 B.C.* (1974). Calls Diodorus's 130 elephants the likeliest figure; calls Porus Alexander's most successful recorded opponent (both cited on Wikipedia).
- J. F. C. Fuller, *The Generalship of Alexander the Great* (1958/1960). Military analysis; judged Diodorus's ~1,000 Macedonian dead "more realistic" than Arrian's; worked out that the crossing would take many hours.

- Frank L. Holt, *Alexander the Great and the Mystery of the Elephant Medallions* (2003). On the "Porus" coins.
- Jona Lendering, Livius.org, "Hydaspes (326 BCE)": https://www.livius.org/articles/battle/hydaspes/ (argues it was really a large skirmish, with Alexander using about one sixth of his forces; site near modern Jhelum town).
- S. Sairam, *The Battle of the Hydaspes: Alexander's last battle* (MA by Research thesis, University of Leeds, 2022): https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/32067/ . Very useful for this video. ### Indian sources and popular claims
- **No contemporary Indian source.** No Sanskrit, Pali or Prakrit text of the period names Alexander, Porus or the battle. That is true, but Indian historical writing of the period does not survive in the form of chronicles at all. Silence counts for neither side. Within about 60 years, Ashoka's Rock Edict XIII does name Greek kings of his own day (Antiochus, Ptolemy and others), but not Alexander.
- **"Porus" as an Indian name.** The Greek *Pōros* is usually taken as a title from *Paurava*, "king of the Purus" (a Vedic tribe; Witzel's suggestion, cited on Wikipedia). "Purushottam" is a modern name. No ancient source gives it (it is used, for example, in the 2017 Sony TV series *Porus*).
- **"Ambhi"** for the king of Taxila is also a modern reconstruction. The Greeks call him Taxiles (a title) and Curtius names him Omphis (Diodorus: Mophis).
- **Where "Porus won" comes from** (Sairam thesis, pp. 18-19):
  - 1941 film *Sikandar* (Sohrab Modi as Porus, Prithviraj Kapoor as Alexander) gave Porus anti-colonial meaning, but it still showed Alexander winning.
  - Soviet Marshal Georgy Zhukov, in India in 1957, reportedly told Indian officers that Alexander was defeated in India. No transcript is available. Still repeated today, e.g. N. S. Rajaram on Bharata Bharati (2012): https://bharatabharati.in/2012/08/02/king-porus-defeat-of-alexander-at-the-battle-of-the-hydaspes-jhelum-n-s-rajaram/
  - D. B. Pandey (1971, a reading of the medallion), Buddha Prakash (*Poros*, 1951; *Poros the Great*, 1994), and Pakistani lawyer-politician Aitzaz Ahsan (*The Indus Saga*, 1997). Sairam says Prakash and Pandey leaned on the Ethiopic Alexander Romance and the *Shahnameh*. Both of those are late legends, and in both Alexander still wins (in the Greek Romance and the *Shahnameh* he kills Porus in single combat).
  - "Ethiopian texts" (E. A. W. Budge's 1896 translations of Ethiopic Alexander legends) are often cited on social media as saying Porus spared a fallen Alexander. I could not find that scene in any checkable source, and the Ethiopic Romance descends from the Greek Romance, in which Alexander kills Porus. Treat it as unverified, not as evidence.
  - Plutarch 62.1 ("the combat with Porus blunted their courage and stayed their further advance") is real and is the strongest genuine text the "Porus won" side quotes.
- Counter-analyses: Tales of Times Forgotten (Spencer McDaniel): https://talesoftimesforgotten.com/2019/11/17/did-king-porus-defeat-alexander-the-great/ ; Ancient History of Punjab: https://panjabhistory.com/porus/

## Fact sheet
Tags: **Arr.** Arrian, **Plut.** Plutarch *Alex.*, **Diod.** Diodorus, **Curt.** Curtius, **Just.** Justin, **Mod.** modern scholarship (named).

**Before the battle**
1. Alexander crossed into the Indus lands in late 327/early 326 BCE after his campaigns in Bactria and Sogdiana. [Arr. 4.22, 5.3; BazBattles cites late 327]
2. The king of Taxila, called Taxiles by the Greeks (Omphis in Curtius; "Ambhi" in modern Indian books), had already offered his submission while Alexander was in Sogdiana, and handed over his kingdom when Alexander arrived. [Diod. 17.86.4-7; Arr. 5.3.5] He sent gifts including about 30 elephants and 700 horsemen [Arr. 5.3.5]. Taxiles was Porus's enemy [Arr. 5.18.6-7; Curt. 9.3.22, later reconciled by marriage].
3. Alexander summoned Porus to come and meet him at his frontier. Porus answered that he would meet him there, but armed. [Curt. 8.13.2]
4. Porus's ally Abisares (Embisarus in Diodorus) was marching to join him, about 400 stadia away, which pushed Alexander to fight quickly. [Diod. 17.87.2-3; Arr. 5.20.5]
5. Porus held the east bank with his army and elephants opposite Alexander's camp. Alexander judged a crossing there impossible: the horses would panic at the elephants, and men climbing out of the water would be cut down. [Arr. 5.9.1-2, 5.10.1-2]
6. The river was in flood: "swollen and turbid", with heavy rain falling and snow melting in the mountains. Arrian dates this to around the summer solstice. [Arr. 5.9.4-5]
7. **The deception, part 1:** Alexander had grain brought into camp from all sides, so Porus would think he meant to wait until winter when the river fell. [Arr. 5.9.3-4]
8. **The deception, part 2:** night after night his cavalry rode along the bank shouting war cries, as if about to cross. Porus marched his elephants to face each noise, until he decided the alarms were empty and stayed in camp, posting lookouts along the bank. [Arr. 5.10.3-4; Plut. 60.2 (from Alexander's letters)]
9. **The double:** Curtius says Alexander dressed Attalus, a man of his own age and similar in face and build, in royal clothes and left him in the camp, so Porus would think the king had not moved. Only Curtius tells this. [Curt. 8.13.21]
10. Curtius also tells of two young Macedonians, Hegesimachus and Nicanor, who led a reckless raid onto a river island and were killed. [Curt. 8.13.13-16]
11. Craterus stayed at the main camp with a large part of the army, including 5,000 Indian allies. His orders: cross only if Porus moved his elephants away. [Arr. 5.11.3-4]
12. The crossing point was a wooded headland with an uninhabited island in front of it, 150 stadia (about 27 km / 17 miles) from the main camp. [Arr. 5.11.1-2] Livius puts it upstream, possibly near the modern Mangla Dam. [Mod. Lendering]
13. Boats, including thirty-oared galleys, had been brought overland in sections and reassembled in the woods. Hides were stuffed with hay to make floats. [Arr. 5.12.3-4; Livius credits Coenus with bringing the boats from the Indus]

**The crossing**
14. In the night "a furious storm of rain" broke out. Thunder and rain drowned out the noise of weapons and orders. [Arr. 5.12.3] Plutarch (from Alexander's letters) also has a stormy night crossing. [Plut. 60.2-3]
15. They landed on what they took to be the far bank, but it was another island. They had to find a ford. The water came up over the infantry's chests, and only the horses' heads stayed above it. [Arr. 5.13.2-3]
16. Onesicritus says that in the crossing Alexander called out: "O Athenians, can you believe what dangers I am running to win your praise?" Plutarch says Alexander's own letters do not say this. [Plut. 60.4] Flag it as an anecdote.
17. Alexander's striking force: about 5,000 cavalry and not much under 6,000 infantry. [Arr. 5.14.1] Fuller adds Craterus's men for the full army.
18. **Porus's son.** The versions differ [Arr. 5.14.3-6]:
    - Aristobulus: the son came with about 60 chariots, before the crossing was finished.
    - Ptolemy (whom Arrian follows): he came with 2,000 cavalry and 120 chariots.
    - "Some writers": a bigger force; the son wounded Alexander and killed Bucephalus.
    - Plutarch (from the letters): 1,000 horsemen and 60 chariots routed. [Plut. 60.5]
    - Curtius instead names Porus's brother Spitaces, with 4,000 cavalry and 100 chariots. [Curt. 8.14.2]
    - Outcome: about 400 Indian cavalry fell, including the son [Arr. 5.15.2]. The chariots stuck in the rain-soaked mud [Arr. 5.15.2; Curt. 8.14.4].

**The battle**
19. Porus left a few elephants and men to watch Craterus and marched against Alexander. [Arr. 5.15.3-4]
20. **Porus's army, by source.** The numbers are not consistent:
    | Source | Infantry | Cavalry | Chariots | Elephants |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Arrian 5.15.4 | 30,000 | 4,000 | 300 | 200 |
    | Diodorus 17.87.2 | 50,000+ | about 3,000 | 1,000+ | 130 |
    | Curtius 8.13.6 | about 30,000 | (not given) | 300 | 85 |
    | Plutarch 62.1 | 20,000 | 2,000 | (not given) | (not given) |
    | Metz Epitome | | | | 85 |
    Modern: Green thinks 130 elephants likeliest; Bosworth thinks no more than about 50; Sairam prefers Curtius's 85. When Eudemus took Porus's elephants years later, he had 120. [Diod. 19.14.8] (Wikipedia's infobox gives "1,000 chariots" citing Plutarch 60.5, but that passage is about the son's force. The 1,000+ figure is Diodorus's.)
21. **Deployment.** Elephants in front, "not less than a plethron" apart (about 100 Greek feet, roughly 30 m) [Arr. 5.15.5], with infantry behind and between them, and cavalry and chariots on both wings [Arr. 5.15.5-7]. Diodorus puts the elephants at equal intervals with infantry between them, so the line looked like a city wall with towers [Diod. 17.87.4-5; same image in Curt. 8.14.13]. Polyaenus gives about 50 feet (about 15 m) [via Sairam]. Arrian's figures (200 elephants about 30 m apart) would make the elephant line about 6 km long, which modern writers find too long. [Mod. Sairam; Lane Fox; English]
22. **Cavalry battle.** Alexander opened with horse archers against the Indian left. He attacked that wing himself and sent Coenus with two cavalry regiments toward the Indian right, with orders to follow the Indian cavalry when it moved. When the Indian cavalry rode across to help their left, Coenus came in behind them, so they had to face two ways. [Arr. 5.16.2-4, 5.17.1-2] The exact route Coenus took is debated. [Mod. Hamilton 1956]
23. **Elephants.** They charged the Macedonian cavalry and phalanx. Diodorus describes men crushed in their armour, lifted by trunks and dashed down, and run through by tusks. [Diod. 17.88.1] The Macedonians gave way before them and then hit the drivers and the animals with javelins and arrows. [Arr. 5.17.3] Curtius says they cut at the elephants' feet with axes and at their trunks with curved swords (kopides). [Curt. 8.14.28-29] Diodorus says the Macedonians used their long spears (sarissas) against the infantry beside the elephants. [Diod. 17.88.2]
24. Wounded elephants with their drivers killed ran wild and trampled friend and foe. Arrian says the Indians, hemmed in among them, suffered worst. [Arr. 5.17.5-6; Diod. 17.88.3] Alexander then locked his infantry's shields and advanced in close order. Plutarch says the fight lasted until the eighth hour. [Arr. 5.17.7; Plut. 60.5]
25. Meanwhile Craterus and the other commanders crossed and joined the pursuit. [Arr. 5.18.1]
26. **Porus fought on.** Diodorus says that with about 40 elephants still under control he counter-attacked, throwing javelins hard enough to rival catapult bolts, until archers concentrated on him and he collapsed from blood loss. [Diod. 17.88.4-6] Arrian says he did not flee as Darius had at Issus and Gaugamela. He kept fighting while any part of his army held, and turned away only when wounded in the right shoulder, the one part of him his armour did not cover. [Arr. 5.18.4-5] Curtius has nine wounds. [Curt. 8.14.32] Plutarch says his elephant drew the spears out of him with its trunk. [Plut. 60.6]
27. Alexander sent Taxiles to call him back. Porus nearly speared him. Alexander then sent several others, last of all Meroes, an old friend of Porus. Porus stopped to drink and came. [Arr. 5.18.6-8]

**The meeting**
28. Alexander rode out, admired his height and looks, and saw that he was not cowed but came "as one brave man would meet another". [Arr. 5.19.1]
29. **The words, by source:**
    - **Arrian 5.19.2:** Alexander asked how he wished to be treated. Porus: "Treat me, Alexander, like a king" (Chinnock: "in a kingly way"). Alexander: that would be done for Alexander's own sake; what did Porus want for his own sake? Porus said everything was included in that one request.
    - **Plutarch 60.7:** asked how he should be treated, Porus said "like a king"; asked if he had anything else to say, he said everything was included in "like a king".
    - **Curtius 8.14.41-45** is quite different. Alexander asks what madness made him fight. Porus says he had thought himself the stronger, now knows he is second, and tells Alexander to act as "this day" advises, the day that showed how fragile good fortune is.
    - The popular line "Treat me as a king treats another king" is **not in any ancient source**. It is a modern expansion, used for example by BazBattles and StudyIQ.
30. **Restored and enlarged.** Alexander let Porus rule his own people and added more land than he had before. [Arr. 5.19.3] Plutarch: he was made a satrap and the added land held 15 nations and 5,000 towns. [Plut. 60.8] Curtius: received among Alexander's friends, then given a bigger kingdom. [Curt. 8.14.45] Diodorus: made king over his old land "in recognition of his valour". [Diod. 17.89.6] After the Hyphasis, his rule ran to that river. [Arr. 5.29.2]
31. **Date.** Arrian: archonship of Hegemon at Athens, month Munychion (about April-May 326 BCE). [Arr. 5.19.3] Arrian also says it was near the summer solstice [5.9.4]. Diodorus's archon year (Chremes) begins in July 326, and some modern scholars (Fontana) put the battle around July. [Diod. 17.87.1 with Loeb note] Most modern accounts say May 326 BCE.

**Casualties**
32. | Source | Indian dead | Macedonian dead |
    |---|---|---|
    | Arrian 5.18.2-3 | nearly 20,000 infantry, 3,000 cavalry; two sons of Porus; all chariots broken | about 80 of 6,000 infantry, 10 horse archers, about 20 Companions, about 200 other cavalry (about 310 in all) |
    | Diodorus 17.89.1-3 | 12,000+ (including two sons of Porus), 9,000+ captured, 80 elephants taken | 280 cavalry and 700+ infantry |
    Arrian's Macedonian losses are suspiciously low for a battle where elephants broke into the phalanx, and both lists come from the winners. Fuller and others prefer Diodorus's roughly 1,000. Arrian's 23,000 Indian dead is close to Diodorus's dead plus captured (21,000), so Arrian may count prisoners as killed. [Mod. Fuller via Wikipedia]

**After the battle**
33. **Bucephalus died here.** Arrian: of exhaustion and old age, about 30 years old, not of wounds. [Arr. 5.19.4-5] Plutarch: most writers say of wounds, some time after the battle; Onesicritus says old age. [Plut. 61.1] Diodorus: died "in the battle against Porus". [Diod. 17.95.5] "Some writers": killed by Porus's son at the crossing. [Arr. 5.14.4]
34. Alexander founded two cities: Nicaea ("Victory") on the battlefield and Bucephala at the crossing, named for the horse. [Arr. 5.19.4; Diod. 17.89.6, 17.95.5] Monsoon rain had damaged both by the time the army came back. [Arr. 5.29.5]
35. **The Porus medallion.** A rare silver decadrachm, probably struck at Babylon around 325-322 BCE (the mint is uncertain): a horseman (usually read as Alexander on Bucephalus) attacks an elephant carrying two men, often read as Porus and a driver. The reverse shows Alexander holding a thunderbolt, crowned by Victory. [British Museum 1926,0402.1; Mod. Holt 2003] The duel shown on it is not described in the battle accounts, so the image is symbolic. [Spink, "Some Talk of Alexander"] Lendering reads the thunderbolt as Alexander claiming credit for the storm.
36. Porus marched with Alexander and brought elephants and about 5,000 men to the siege of Sangala. [Arr. 5.24.4]

**The Hyphasis (Beas), 326 BCE**
37. At the Hyphasis the men heard that beyond the river lay rich land, brave fighters and more elephants than anywhere else in India. [Arr. 5.25.1] Plutarch and Diodorus report the Ganges kingdoms (the Nanda realm) as having vast armies: Plutarch says 80,000 horse, 200,000 foot, 8,000 chariots and 6,000 elephants [Plut. 62.2]; Diodorus says 20,000 horse, 200,000 foot, 2,000 chariots and 4,000 elephants under King Xandrames [Diod. 17.93.2]. These are rumours as reported, not counts.
38. Morale had broken: almost eight years of campaigning, worn-out horses and gear, Greek clothes gone, and 70 days of monsoon rain with thunder. [Diod. 17.94.1-3]
39. Alexander spoke to the officers. Coenus answered for the army: the men were few and worn out and wanted to see home, and Alexander should go back and raise new armies. [Arr. 5.26-27] The speeches are Arrian's reconstruction.
40. Alexander shut himself in his tent for three days. Then the sacrifices for the crossing were unfavourable (Ptolemy's account), and he announced the return. The men wept and cheered. [Arr. 5.28.1-5, 5.29.1] Arrian comments that this was the only defeat Alexander ever took, and it came from his own men. [Arr. 5.29.1; K&G paraphrase]
41. Plutarch's verdict: the struggle with Porus "blunted their courage and stayed their further advance into India". [Plut. 62.1]
42. Twelve great altars were set up at the Hyphasis to mark the limit of the march. [Arr. 5.29.1; Diod. 17.95.1]
43. Coenus died soon after, of illness. [Arr. 6.2.1] Some modern writers find the timing suspicious, but no ancient source accuses Alexander.
44. Plutarch says Androcottus (Chandragupta Maurya), who had seen Alexander as a young man, later said Alexander nearly took the country because its king (the Nanda) was hated. [Plut. 62.4]
45. Alexander did not go straight home. He sailed down the Jhelum and Indus, fought the Malli and was nearly killed by an arrow wound to the chest at their town (325 BCE). He crossed the Gedrosian desert and died at Babylon in June 323 BCE, more than two years after the Hydaspes. [Arr. 6.9-11, 7.25-28] Claims that he died "on the way home from wounds Porus gave him" are wrong.
46. **Porus's end.** He was confirmed in his kingdom after Alexander's death [Diod. 18.39.6]. Later the Macedonian officer Eudemus "treacherously" killed him and took his elephants. Eudemus joined Eumenes in 317 BCE with 120 elephants. [Diod. 19.14.8] So Porus died between about 321 and 317 BCE; the exact year is unknown.
47. Within about 20 years Chandragupta Maurya held the Punjab. Seleucus gave up the eastern lands for 500 elephants (c. 305-303 BCE). This is known from Greek sources (Strabo 15.2.9; Appian, *Syriaca* 55), not Indian ones. [Mod. general]

## The "who won?" question

**The battle: Alexander, clearly.** Every ancient account, the vulgate writers included, has the Indian army broken, Porus wounded and captured, and his sons dead. On the evidence we have, the dispute is about the price of the win, not who won it:
- The physical record fits a win: two cities, one named Nicaea ("Victory"), founded on Porus's land; victory medallions struck within about three years; Macedonian garrisons and satraps (Philip, later Eudemus) in Punjab.
- The army went on fighting east of the Jhelum, at Sangala with Porus himself on its side, and reached the Beas.
- Sparing a brave king and keeping him as a client fits Alexander's habit: Mazaeus, Mithrenes, Phrataphernes, Taxiles himself. It was cheap control of a hard country. Even in the legends the revisionists cite (the *Alexander Romance*, the *Shahnameh*), Alexander wins. In the Romance he kills Porus.

**The "Porus won" arguments, and how strong each is:**
| Argument | Fair point? | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| "All sources are Greek/Roman, written centuries later" | Yes. True and important | They draw on several eyewitnesses who did not all agree with each other, and none of them has a defeat. Silence from the Indian side cuts both ways: no Indian text says Porus won either. |
| "Alexander gave him his kingdom back, and more. A winner wouldn't" | Partly. It shows Porus was strong and useful | Alexander did this often. Porus became a subordinate who marched on Alexander's campaigns. |
| "The army turned back right after" | Yes. Plutarch 62.1 links the hard fight to the loss of nerve | The turn came weeks later and several rivers further east, at the Beas, after more conquests. The reasons the sources give: eight years of war, monsoon, worn-out gear, fear of the Nanda armies. |
| "Bucephalus died, so Alexander must have lost" | No | A horse's death says nothing about the result. Sources differ on the cause (old age vs wounds). |
| "Alexander died of wounds from Porus" | No | His near-fatal wound was from the Malli in 325. He died in Babylon in 323. |
| "Zhukov / Russian / Chinese / Ethiopian / Persian sources say Alexander lost" | No | Zhukov's 1957 remark has no published source. No Chinese text of the period mentions Alexander. The Ethiopic and Persian texts are medieval legends in which Alexander wins. |
| "Taxila university was nearby and didn't record it" | No | It assumes Taxila kept chronicles we would have. No such records survive for any event of that time. |
| "Indian sources record Seleucus but not Alexander" | No | Seleucus's treaty with Chandragupta is also known only from Greek and Roman writers. |

**Why Alexander turned back (modern consensus):** exhaustion after eight years and many thousands of miles, the monsoon (70 days of rain, according to Diodorus), wear on men, horses and gear, reports of the huge Nanda armies beyond, and the hard fight against Porus as a warning of what more Indian kingdoms might cost. The army refused, not an enemy. Historians differ on whether to call it a "mutiny"; Brice calls it military unrest.

**Who came out ahead?** A fair answer has two parts. Alexander won the battle. Porus kept his throne, his kingdom grew to the Beas, his rival Taxiles had to make peace with him, and he outlasted Alexander. Sairam (2022) argues both can be called victors. That is a respectable scholarly view and different from "Porus won the battle".

**Recommended payoff wording (draft):**
> "So who really won? On the field, Alexander. Every account we have says the Indian line broke, and that Porus was carried off his elephant bleeding from his wounds. But look at what happened next. The man who lost the battle kept his crown and saw his kingdom grow. The man who won it got no further than a few more rivers before his own army refused to follow him. Alexander won the Hydaspes, and India ended his march. Both of those are true."

Keep "India ended his march" as framing. The sources show the army's refusal, which Plutarch links in part to the fight with Porus. Do not say Porus forced the retreat.
