## HOOK

[S01] A line of war elephants stands on the bank of a flooded river in Punjab. On the biggest one sits an Indian king. Across the water, in the rain, waits Alexander the Great.

[S02] Ask the Greek writers who won here, and they say Alexander. Ask millions of Indians online, and many say Porus. So, who really won? The evidence gives a stranger answer than either side expects.

[S03] The Hydaspes river, called the Jhelum today, in Punjab. Early summer, three twenty-six BC. On the east bank is King Porus, with somewhere between eighty-five and two hundred war elephants, depending on which ancient writer you read.

[S04] On the west bank is Alexander, king of Macedon, twenty-nine years old. He has never lost a battle. But the river is in flood, and horses will not face elephants. He cannot cross here. It starts with a king who said yes.

## SEGMENT 1: TWO KINGS

[S05] Picture a map, with north at the top. In eight years, Alexander has marched from Greece across the whole Persian Empire. Now he comes down from the mountains into the rich river country of north-west India.

[S06] The first great city is Taxila. Its king, called Taxiles by the Greeks, hands over his kingdom without a fight and sends Alexander gifts, among them thirty elephants and seven hundred horsemen. He also has an enemy across the river.

[S07] Meet Porus. He rules the land between the Jhelum and the next river east. The Greeks call him Porus, probably from Paurava, king of the Puru people. The name Purushottam, used in many films today, comes from no ancient source.

[S08] Greek writers say he is huge, well over six feet tall, and they write of him with respect. On our map, his kingdom is gold, and the land Alexander holds is blue.

[S09] Alexander sends word for Porus to come and meet him at the border. One Roman writer, Curtius, records the answer. Porus will meet him at the border, yes. Armed.

## SEGMENT 2: THE BLUFF

[S10] Now look at the river. Snow is melting in the mountains, and the summer rain has begun. The Jhelum is wide, fast and brown. On the far bank, Porus has lined up his elephants opposite the Greek camp.

[S11] So Alexander plays a game. He has grain brought into camp from every side, so Porus will think he means to wait for winter, when the river falls.

[S12] Then, night after night, his horsemen ride along the bank shouting war cries, as if about to cross. Each time, Porus marches his elephants to meet the noise. After a while, he stops bothering.

[S13] Curtius adds one more trick. A man called Attalus, about the king's age and build, is dressed in Alexander's royal clothes and left in the camp. Only Curtius tells this story. Whatever the truth, the real Alexander is about to leave.

## SEGMENT 3: THE STORM

[S14] About seventeen miles upstream, a wooded headland faces an island. There, boats carried overland in pieces are put back together. In the night, a violent storm breaks. Thunder and rain cover the noise of men and weapons.

[S15] About eleven thousand men cross with Alexander. They land as day breaks, and find they are on another island. Ptolemy, one of his generals, was there, and remembered the next part.

[S16] They must wade the last channel. The water comes up over the soldiers' chests. Of the horses, only their heads show above the river. If Porus finds them now, they are trapped in the water.

[S17] Porus does find out. He sends his son ahead with chariots and horsemen. The ancient writers disagree on how many: sixty chariots, or a hundred and twenty, with up to two thousand horsemen.

[S18] It is too late. The Greeks are on dry land. The heavy chariots stick in the rain-soaked mud. About four hundred Indian horsemen fall, and the son of Porus dies with them.

## SEGMENT 4: THE WALL OF ELEPHANTS

[S19] Now Porus must choose. One Greek army is still across the river, in the camp. Another is marching toward him. He leaves a few elephants to watch the camp, and turns his army to face Alexander.

[S20] He sets his elephants along the front, spaced like towers in a city wall. Behind and between them stands his infantry: thirty thousand, says Arrian. Other writers give anywhere from twenty to fifty thousand. Horsemen and chariots hold both wings.

[S21] Alexander keeps his horses away from the elephants and strikes at the Indian cavalry on one wing. Then his general Coenus rides around behind them. The Indian horsemen are caught between two attacks and pushed back toward their own elephants.

## SEGMENT 5: THE ELEPHANTS

[S22] And now the elephants charge. Diodorus writes that men are crushed in their armour, lifted by trunks and thrown down, and run through by tusks. The Macedonian line gives way.

[S23] The Macedonians strike back at the drivers and the animals with javelins and arrows. Curtius says they cut at the elephants' feet with axes and at their trunks with curved swords. The fight goes on for hours.

[S24] Wounded elephants, their drivers dead, turn and run wild. They trample anyone in their path. Arrian says the Indians, packed in among them, suffer worst. Then the Greek camp crosses the river and joins the attack.

## SEGMENT 6: THE KING WHO STAYED

[S25] Think of Darius, the Persian king Alexander beat before. He ran from Alexander twice. Arrian writes that Porus does not run. He fights from his elephant while any part of his army still holds.

[S26] He is wounded in the right shoulder, the one place his armour does not cover, says Arrian. Curtius gives him nine wounds. Plutarch says his elephant pulls the spears out of him with its trunk.

[S27] Alexander sends Taxiles, the king who said yes, to call him in. Porus nearly spears him. Then Alexander sends an old friend of Porus, called Meroes. Porus stops, drinks some water, and comes.

## SEGMENT 7: "LIKE A KING"

[S28] Alexander rides out to meet him. Arrian says Porus comes as one brave man would meet another. Alexander asks how he wishes to be treated. Porus answers: "Treat me, Alexander, like a king."

[S29] Alexander asks if he wants anything else. Everything, Porus says, is in that one request. The famous longer version, "as a king treats a king", is in no ancient source. Alexander gives Porus back his kingdom, and adds more land to it.

[S30] The winners counted the dead. Arrian gives about three hundred Macedonians and twenty-three thousand Indians. Diodorus gives about a thousand Macedonians. Several historians find Arrian's number far too low.

[S31] Here Bucephalus, Alexander's horse since boyhood, dies, of wounds or of old age; the sources argue. Alexander founds two cities: Bucephala, for the horse, and Nicaea, a Greek name meaning "Victory".

## SEGMENT 8: THE RIVER HE NEVER CROSSED

[S32] Alexander marches on east, and Porus marches with him, bringing elephants and five thousand men. Then, a few weeks later, they reach another river, the Hyphasis, known today as the Beas.

[S33] Beyond it, rumours say, lie the kingdoms of the Ganges with armies far bigger than Porus ever had. Diodorus says the rain has fallen for seventy days. The men have marched for eight years. Their clothes and gear are worn out.

[S34] They refuse to go on. Coenus, the general who rode behind the Indian horse, speaks for them. Alexander shuts himself in his tent for three days. Then the omens turn bad, and he gives the order to turn back.

[S35] Arrian calls it the only defeat Alexander ever suffered, at the hands of his own men. Plutarch writes something the Indian side quotes often: the fight with Porus "blunted their courage and stayed their further advance into India."

## SEGMENT 9: MYTHS

[S36] Some popular claims do not hold up. Alexander does not die from wounds Porus gave him. He is nearly killed by an arrow a year later, in another Indian town, and dies in Babylon in three twenty-three BC.

[S37] The Ethiopian and Persian texts often quoted for Porus are legends written many centuries later, and in them Alexander still wins. The letter about every Indian mother bearing an Alexander comes from no ancient source.

## ENDING

[S38] So, who really won? On the field, Alexander. Every account we have says the Indian line broke, and Porus left the fight wounded. No Indian record from the time mentions the battle either way.

[S39] But look at what happened next. The man who lost the battle kept his crown and saw his kingdom grow to the Beas. The man who won it got a few rivers further, then his own army turned him around.

[S40] Porus outlives Alexander. A few years later, a Macedonian officer murders him and takes his elephants. Within about twenty years, Chandragupta Maurya rules this land. Alexander won the Hydaspes, and India ended his march. Both are true.

[S41] A flooded river. A wall of elephants. A king who asked only to be treated like one. And a river further east that Alexander never crossed.
