# Growth and Scripting Insights (read before every new script)

Source: three "start a channel from scratch and go viral" videos by Jack Craig (YouTube growth coach), distilled 2026-10-21.
1. **Broke Blocks**: AI animated Roblox "Grow a Garden" Shorts, 7-day challenge.
2. **Street Dog Salon**: AI pet-grooming Shorts in the style of the "Brazen" channel, 24-hour challenge.
3. **Isa's channel**: a beginner repurposing viral videos with "value-add editing" (Nile Blue clips), 7-day challenge, monetized on day 19.

**Caveat for BattleField:** all three examples are **Shorts** (40-60 s). Our videos are **10-13 minute long-form** history. The *principles* (idea selection, structure, hook, retention, packaging, upload rhythm) carry over. The numbers (swipe rate, 3-second rule, 1-3 s clips, upload gaps) are Shorts-specific. Each section below is marked **[Applies]** or **[Shorts only]** where it matters.

---

## 1. The pre-script checklist (use this every time)

Before writing a single line of a new battle script, answer these:

- [ ] **Proven idea?** Is there evidence this battle/angle already pulls views (similar videos with strong view counts, comment-heavy outliers)? We improve what works; we don't invent from nothing.
- [ ] **Universally relatable?** Can someone with zero history knowledge understand the stakes in one sentence? (US and India audience.) Prefer a human hook (underdog, betrayal, absurd odds, a mistake) over names and dates.
- [ ] **Emotional hook named?** One emotion the viewer feels from the first 15 s: awe, absurdity, "no way", injustice, dread, pride. Write it down.
- [ ] **Completion compulsion?** Does the opening promise something the viewer can only get by finishing (a reveal, a twist, an outcome)?
- [ ] **Open-loop hook?** The first lines ask a question or plant a mystery; they do NOT summarize the answer.
- [ ] **Conflict arc mapped?** Hook > rising action > conflict > comeback > rising action > payoff, with real timestamps or beats of the history in each slot (see section 3).
- [ ] **Not predictable?** Is there a genuine turn (a miscalculation, betrayal, storm, reversal) in the middle, not just a linear march to the result?
- [ ] **Realistic and true?** No exaggerated, gimmicky claims. Dispute-hedging in the narration itself (already a rule in `CLAUDE.md`).
- [ ] **Brand-consistent?** Same format, voice and look as the channel's other videos so the channel builds a recognizable identity.
- [ ] **Hook visual planned?** The first frame has motion or a striking image, not a title card or a static establishing shot.
- [ ] **Packaging ready?** Title formula, thumbnail idea and description drafted *before* the video is finished (section 6).

## 2. What all three videos agree on (the common core)

1. **Ideas are improved, not invented.** "Original ideas don't win on YouTube. Better execution does." Find a proven concept, then make it your own with a twist. Never clone an established channel 1:1 (YouTube doesn't reward duplicates).
2. **Study winners in a structured way.** For the top 3 videos in a niche, write down: title, views, and for each scene its duration, visual description, script line, and a screenshot. 18-20 minutes of this work reveals the hidden formula. Then feed the notes to an AI to find patterns.
3. **Every video follows a repeatable phase structure.** Brazen: declare > assess > isolate > process > build > reveal. Garden Shorts: hook > rising action > payoff. Looped/conflict arc: hook > rise > conflict > comeback > rise > payoff. A structure, filled in with specifics, beats improvising.
4. **Three selection criteria for a topic:**
   - **Universal relatability**: no niche knowledge needed (food, pets, everyday life).
   - **Emotional hook**: a feeling that stops the scroll (absurdity, shock, awe).
   - **Completion compulsion**: an opening promise that is only resolved at the end.
   (Video 3 reframes the third as *open loop*: a question the viewer must see answered.)
5. **Hook = the most important 3 seconds (Shorts) / 15 seconds (long-form).** Movement in the first frame; a question instead of a statement.
6. **Predictability kills retention.** Adding a conflict and a comeback is what makes a story un-skippable.
7. **Retention metrics are the real signal.** High average percentage viewed (80%+ on Shorts) and high "stayed to watch" rate (swipe-through, 80%+) make YouTube push the video further. Retention that *rises* as the push widens is the strongest sign.
8. **Don't rely on AI to do it for you.** First AI output is usually bad. Edit the prompt, add reference images, iterate. The human supplies taste, structure and idea; AI supplies production speed.
9. **Don't over-invest in channel art.** Name, avatar and banner (about 5-10 minutes) do not drive views. Spend the time on ideas and videos.
10. **Patience between uploads.** Never flood the algorithm. Give each video room to run before uploading the next (see section 7).
11. **The first upload is a lottery; from the second upload there are "no excuses".** First videos on new channels often get few views (3,000, 27,000, even 70,000) and the 2nd then explodes (10 million). Do not pivot or quit after one weak video.
12. **Algorithm behavior is a rollercoaster.** Views come in "pushes" (test batches), flatten, then another push may come hours or days later. Staying level-headed and not tweaking in panic is part of the process.
13. **Packaging is simple and descriptive.** Plain title that says what happens + two emojis; simple description; "not made for kids"; a few relevant tags; custom thumbnail with a clean first frame.
14. **Be systematic: nothing left up to luck.** Base decisions on proven data (competitor videos with millions of views), not gut.
15. **Speed.** Ideation 11-45 minutes, production 1-2 hours. The process is built to avoid analysis paralysis: get a high-level structure, then fill the gaps while making the video.
16. **Be honest about AI/"inauthentic content".** YouTube demonetizes content with minimal variation across uploads or that is trivially replicable at scale. Heavy human creative direction, a unique script, commentary and story are what keep a channel safe and monetizable. Keep realistic themes; gimmicky novelty niches die fast and can't build a brand.

## 3. Story structures (use these as the skeleton of every script)

### 3a. Basic arc (what beginners do, predictable)
Hook > Rising action > Payoff.

### 3b. Conflict arc (default for developed niches)
Hook > Rising action > **Conflict** > **Comeback** > Rising action > Payoff.
The conflict and comeback make the story less predictable, which raises engagement and views.

### 3c. Looped arc (two conflicts)
Hook > Rise 1 > Conflict 1 > Comeback 1 > Rise 2 (often sparked by a call to action) > Conflict 2 > Payoff (revenge or resolution).

### 3d. Mapping to a battle (our use)
| Slot | What it is in a battle video |
|---|---|
| Hook | A question or an impossible-sounding claim, plus a striking opening image. Do NOT state the outcome. |
| Rising action | Background, forces, preparations, the plan; every beat raises the stakes |
| Conflict | The thing that goes wrong: a miscalculation, a storm, a betrayal, an ambush, a lost position |
| Comeback | The clever or desperate answer that turns it around (or the tragic response, if the battle is a loss) |
| Rising action 2 | The final push, the decisive move |
| Payoff | The outcome, the consequence, the lasting meaning |

Rules from the examples:
- Map **real timestamps/beats from the history** into each slot (the way Jack noted "halfway through, he miscalculated the weight" = conflict).
- Leave gaps at first; fill them while producing.
- Keep what's proven. If a structure got high retention, reuse it unchanged for the next video ("why change something that's already working?").

### 3e. Brazen "phase breakdown" idea (for a procedural / build-up feel)
Create a **phase table**: duration of each phase, number of scenes, and a script formula per phase. Ask an AI to extract this from the analysis document of the winners. Useful for matching our beat markers `[Sxx]` to phases and for keeping pacing consistent between videos.

## 4. Hooks

- **Open loop, not a summary.** Bad: "This YouTuber turned 100 cans of Red Bull into powder with a drying machine." (answers the question). Good: "What would happen if you compressed 100 cans of Red Bull into one drink?" (plants a question).
- **Declaration hook** (Brazen): "Today we are pinching 99,099 broccoli florets." A bold, specific promise the viewer wants to see completed. In history terms: a specific, startling statement, then the story of how.
- **Motion in the first frame.** Character running at the camera, sudden movement. For BattleField: open on action (a charge, a cannon flash, a wave hitting a hull), not a still wide shot.
- **Build a payoff in the hook that returns at the end.** Street Dog Salon used a "before" Polaroid in the hook and compared it with the final result at the end: the viewer waits for the comparison. History analog: a line or image in the first 15 s that we pay off in the last minute (a flag, a phrase, a number).
- **Spend the extra effort on the first 3 scenes.** The first attempt at the opening scene was "basic"; reworking it made the video. The hook scenes must be perfect.
- **Hook idea list per video:** write 3 hook candidates (question / claim / image), pick the one that makes the viewer ask "wait, how?".
- **[Long-form]** The 15-second rule in `hook-and-length` memory is the long-form version of this.

## 5. Topic and idea selection

1. **Validate the niche before the topic**: look for channels that got huge views in few uploads (e.g., 200M views in under 20 uploads, 100M in first 4 uploads), or high average views per video with *low re-upload competition* (search the titles and see only the original).
2. **Trending signal**: check Google Trends and the news for what the audience is currently into.
3. **Outlier comments are a goldmine.** A video with 16,000 comments when similar videos get 6,000 reveals what the audience cares about (a specific mechanic, character, item). Fold that proven element into your story.
4. **Combine two proven ideas** (a game mechanic from video A + a conflict from channel B) to get something new but validated.
5. **Pick for relatability, not just personal interest.** Choose topics with zero knowledge barrier. Example: a science video that is famous with a niche crowd is a worse pick than a food video. For battles: lead with the human/universal angle even if the battle is obscure.
6. **Choose what you can execute fast** with the tools you have (AI/animation in our case).
7. **Don't chase what you personally love at the cost of reach** for a growth phase; but long-term you need content you actually care about, and a channel dependent on one other channel's supply is fragile (Isa's lesson).
8. **Think in a repeatable format**, so each video reinforces the brand (and algorithm learns your audience). Switching between adjacent topics within the same audience is OK (the example channel switched games and kept views); switching audiences resets the discovery phase.
9. **Competitor analysis template** (use for each reference video):
   - Title / views / duration
   - Per scene: timestamp, duration, visual, script line, screenshot
   - Hook type, conflict point, comeback, payoff
   - Comment themes and outliers
   - What element made it relatable and what emotional hook it used

## 6. Packaging: title, description, thumbnail, tags

- **Title**: describe what happens, plain and relevant (formula borrowed from the top videos of a reference channel; swap in your own words). Add **two emojis**. For a brand-new channel, include the topic/game name once to help the algorithm place you. (Long-form history titles: lean on the question or the odd claim, e.g., "How X beat Y" style; keep accurate.)
- **Description**: restate the title, a bit more detail; 3 hashtags; credit original creators where relevant.
- **Tags**: a handful of relevant ones, not many.
- **Settings**: "Not made for kids"; leave the rest on default.
- **Thumbnail**: custom thumbnails make a channel page look clean and matter once YouTube serves the video on home/suggested (more relevant for long-form than Shorts). Make the first frame of the video thumbnail-worthy. (Shorts custom thumbnail is only editable from the mobile app.)
- **Watch the video back** before uploading to catch issues.
- **Channel basics**: use an account at least ~7 days old with watch history (new accounts can look like spam). Handle taken? Add a suffix (e.g., "real"). Memorable character/brand/persona helps later monetization beyond ads (merch, sponsorships).
- **Consistency of branding**: avatar and banner should match in character/mood (the one annoyance Jack had), but do not obsess.

## 7. Upload cadence and the algorithm

[Mostly Shorts, but the logic helps long-form]
- **First 5 uploads: wait at least 48 hours between uploads** and check the real-time candles. Upload the next only when views fall under ~100 views/hour for 12+ hours straight (small channel). Bigger case: wait until under ~5,000 views/hour for 24+ hours (video 3).
- **Why**: uploading while a video is still being pushed splits the push. Example: video 3 hit 500k views and gained 7,000 subs; rushing video 4 flopped under 1,000 views; after waiting, it hit 13M.
- **Don't flood; give the algorithm one thing to focus on.**
- **Keep the next video ready** so you can upload the moment the current one cools off.
- **Pushes come in waves**: initial test (flat first hours) > bigger push at hours 5-8 > possible flatline > aggressive push the next day. Each flat day can precede a new leg up. Don't touch a video that is working.
- **Re-checking**: check analytics once or twice a day, not every 10 minutes (it's emotionally draining; Jack even hid analytics from Isa while she worked on video 2).
- **Pivot rule**: don't pivot niche in the first 5 uploads unless the audience is clearly the same.
- **Long-form note**: for 10-13 min videos, the cadence is much slower anyway; the principle is "never publish into the shadow of a video that is still climbing".

## 8. Analytics: what to read and what the numbers mean

(All figures are from Shorts; use them as a mental model.)
| Metric | Healthy value | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Swipe-through ("viewed vs swiped away") | ~80%+ (first upload on new channel can be ~61%) | Share of people served the video who stay past ~3 s. Needs 80%+ for virality. Rises as YouTube finds the right audience. |
| Average percentage viewed / avg view duration | 80-88%; e.g., 52 s of 61 s | Retention. If it holds or climbs during wider pushes, the video keeps being pushed. |
| Real-time candles (hourly views, last 48 h) | Pushes appear as bursts | Used both to see pushes and to time the next upload. |
| Comments and like:dislike ratio | Healthy ratio, outlier comments | Comments signal people love/hate it; many comments = deeper engagement. |
| Subscribers | e.g., 1,000+ after 150k-400k views | Conversion about 0.3-0.5% in these examples. |
| Audience / revenue (RPM) | Check after a few days | Demographics (US) estimate revenue per 1,000 views. |

Useful patterns observed:
- First 24 h can be very flat (first 8 hours) then push; a decent number at 24 h (27k) did not guarantee more.
- A video can have 3,000 views at 24 h and 10M by day 10. Judge by retention and swipe-through, not by raw views.
- If metrics are high but views stall, YouTube likely tested twice and may push again; wait.
- Complaints about "it's AI" appear when the story is exaggerated; realistic themes get fewer complaints.
- Monetization (YPP): 1,000 subs + 3M Shorts views (90 days) + uploads; channel got monetized 19 days after start (AdSense approval took ~8 days, review ~1 day). Keep this as background only; check current YouTube thresholds before relying on them (long-form needs 1,000 subs + 4,000 watch hours).

## 9. Production process lessons

- **Ideation first, everything else second.** "It's not the vehicle (niche) that gets you views, it's the videos."
- **Structure on paper, then fill the blanks.** Looped arc + timestamps = the skeleton; scripting is filling the blanks; editing is matching visuals to voice left to right along the timeline.
- **Watch source material and note timestamps** that map to conflict/comeback/payoff.
- **Build scenes before characters** (Street Dog Salon): generate an empty environment image first and use it as a reference image in every later generation. That is how consistency is achieved across scenes. (Matches our Flow/Blender approach: establish the world, then add figures.)
- **Use reference images and character references** in image prompts; edit AI-generated prompts rather than using them raw.
- **Prompt workflow**: describe the scene to ChatGPT > get an image prompt > generate > fix > animate the still with a short, simple motion prompt (e.g., "character running at camera, ground moving").
- **Generate and edit in parallel**: start the next generations while editing, to avoid dead time.
- **Pacing**: cut to 1-3 second clips for Shorts (fast); for long-form, keep a visible change (angle, subject, motion) every few seconds.
- **Sound design matters**: footsteps, character noises, music, SFX make scenes feel alive. Add captions. Graphics to highlight key moments.
- **Voice over**: generate line by line, remove dead air, sync to clips. (Our voice: Algieba. See `CLAUDE.md`.)
- **Add something visual that pays off at the end** (Polaroid-before idea, pinboard of results).
- **Watch it back 2+ times** before uploading and fix issues.
- **Time budgets in the videos**: research 18-20 min, ideation 11-45 min, production 1-2 h, upload 5 min.
- **Edit with a human eye**: AI first drafts of scripts are never perfect; adjust flow and align to the phase structure.
- **Value-add editing** (reusing others' clips): needs commentary, storyline or transformation (not just re-upload). Credit the original creator. Not relevant to us beyond showing why original narration and structure keep a channel monetizable.

## 10. Mindset and lessons (for the long game)

- A first video that "flops" is normal. Both examples that took off had weak or average first uploads.
- Jack's own history: over 50 channels of guessing before the first viral video; the structure/criteria came from that trial and error. Systems beat guessing.
- Quitters lose: Isa nearly gave up at 3,000 views; the same video later hit 10M+.
- Don't compare day-1 numbers with the final result; algorithm timing is unpredictable.
- Stay positive when views stall; the algorithm may test a short multiple times before the big push.
- **Brand and ownership**: a channel that depends on another creator's output (re-uploads) is fragile. Build original content you care about; consider the quick-cash niche as a bridge only.
- AI-enabled content (AI as an extension of human creative vision) is different from AI slop; keep the human direction visible.
- YouTube needs constant new supply (200B Shorts views per day), so the algorithm wants good new content. A clear strategy plus quality gets rewarded.

## 11. Rules for BattleField distilled from the above

1. Every script gets a one-line **emotional hook** and a one-line **open-loop question** written at the top before drafting.
2. Every script is built on a **conflict arc** (at least one conflict and one comeback with real historical beats). Longer videos may use a looped arc (two conflicts).
3. The first 15 seconds: striking motion image + question or bold specific claim. No outcome spoilers.
4. Plant a **hook-to-end payoff** (an image/line from the opening that returns in the last minute).
5. Make the central idea **understandable in one sentence to a non-historian** (US and India audiences).
6. Before writing, do the **competitor structured analysis** of the top 3 videos on the topic (title, views, scene breakdown, comment outliers); store it in `<Battle>/Sources.md` or a `Research.md`.
7. Look for **comment outliers** on competitor videos: what do people argue about or ask for? Answer it in the script.
8. Keep **realistic and true**; hedge disputes; no exaggeration for shock value.
9. Pick the title formula early (plain description + question/claim + two emojis if it suits the channel); draft thumbnail concept with the script.
10. **Let each video run** before publishing the next; keep the next one ready. Don't pivot after one weak upload.
11. After each upload, log in `PROGRESS.md`: views at 24 h / 48 h / 7 days, average percentage viewed, first-3-seconds / swipe-through, comments, and what we'd change. Feed this back into the next script.
12. Reuse what worked: if a structure produced high retention, keep it for the next battle and only change the topic.
